# Shrink partitions, vectorize parsing, halve the 60M run

V2 projects to about **8 s locally (about 9 s on the scorer)** at 60M events. About 3.3 s of that is CSV parsing, about 2.1 s is hash dedup, and about 1.3 s is the session sort. The published evidence points to three independent levers that together could plausibly bring the run to **about 4.5–5.5 s locally**. First, replace the scalar byte loops with an AVX2 structural scanner and fixed-format SWAR/SSE timestamp parsers. These should be worth roughly 0.9–1.6 s combined, because this dialect never puts `,` or `\n` inside quotes, so the expensive quote-aware machinery that general SIMD CSV parsers need drops out. Second, make every hash table and sort unit fit in the 1 MB L2. At 60M, each of V2's 256 dedup tables grows to **about 33.5 MB** and each sort bucket to about 7.4 MB. A local benchmark of the same record layout cut the parallel sort from **470 ms to 105–128 ms** just by shrinking sort units to about 460 KB. Third, a handful of configuration fixes cost an afternoon and are worth about 0.3–0.8 s: Parquet dictionary and statistics off, transparent huge pages for the multi-GB anonymous buffers, pre-sized scatter buffers, and skipping destructors at exit. Two caveats frame everything here. If the scorer's page cache is cold, disk throughput (6.6 s to over 30 s for 5.5 GB on GCP Persistent Disk) outweighs every CPU optimization. And with about 20% machine-to-machine variance, the scorer cannot confirm any single change worth less than about 5%.

## Parsing and memory traffic make up about 8 s at 60M

A straight 3× scale-up of the 20M phase table gives about 3.3 s for links plus event parse, 0.8 s for clicks, 1.95 s for dedup, 1.2 s for sessions and 0.36 s for write. Two phases will scale worse than linearly, because their working sets cross cache boundaries. `hashbrown` reserves 1/8 of slots as empty ([hashbrown raw.rs](https://github.com/rust-lang/hashbrown/blob/master/src/raw.rs)). A 60M bucket holds about 234k events, which is just past the 229,376-entry capacity of a 2^18 table, so `HashMap::with_capacity(total)` ([src/v2/mod.rs:133](../src/v2/mod.rs)) allocates **2^19 slots × 64 B ≈ 33.5 MB per table**, against about 8.4 MB at 20M ([partitioning notes](../research_notes/Rust%20pipeline%20performance%20techniques/partitioning_and_dedup.md)). Both sizes are DRAM-resident on a machine with 1 MB of L2 per core, so nearly every fresh-key insert (97% of rows) is a cache miss plus a 64-byte move. A realistic 60M budget is therefore about **7.7–8.6 s locally**. Add the kernel teardown of 3–6 GB of anonymous memory at exit (about 50–340 ms, bracketed by a measured 280 ms for a 16 GB process ([LKML RFC](https://lkml.iu.edu/2607.2/06062.html)) and 56 ms/GB for a 320 GB munmap ([patchwork](https://patchwork.kernel.org/comment/22057615/))). Then add 100–250 ms of container start that the binary cannot control ([Anjali et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2110.11462)).

The parse phase is slow for a specific reason. V2 runs at about **0.35 GB/s per physical core end to end, roughly 660 cycles per row**. It touches each byte up to three times: a scalar `position` for the newline ([src/v2/scan.rs:37](../src/v2/scan.rs)), a per-byte comma loop, and a `contains(b'"')` rescan per field ([src/csv.rs:48–80](../src/csv.rs)). Timestamps are parsed digit by digit with an `Option` return per digit pair ([src/ts.rs:219](../src/ts.rs)). For comparison, the Rust `csv` crate's byte-at-a-time DFA manages about 0.7 GB/s per thread on Zen 5, and csveee's SIMD parser manages **1.8 GB/s with quoting and 2.1 GB/s without** ([csveee, PVLDB 2026](https://db.in.tum.de/~ellmann/papers/csveee.pdf)).

One code-level observation explains an earlier puzzle: raising `SHOPT_BUCKET_BITS` from 8 to 10 made no measurable difference at 20M (PLAN §6). The dedup phase allocates `vec![Vec::new(); nb]` output vectors *per event bucket* ([src/v2/mod.rs:149](../src/v2/mod.rs)), so the person scatter creates **nb² growing Vecs**. That is 65,536 at 8 bits and about a million at 10 bits. Any cache benefit from smaller buckets was likely eaten by allocation and doubling churn. Smaller buckets therefore need a different scatter mechanism, not just a bigger `nb`. That is the theme of the third section below.

## Vectorized scanning and fixed-format parsing attack the 3.3 s parse phase

General SIMD CSV parsers spend their cleverness on quotes. They compute the prefix XOR of the quote mask, often with PCLMULQDQ, to learn which delimiters sit inside quoted regions ([Langdale & Lemire](https://arxiv.org/html/1902.08318v7)). This dialect forbids `,`, `"` and `\n` inside quotes, so **every comma and newline is structural**. That puts the scanner in csveee's cheaper "unquoted mode", where "no prefix XOR needs to be computed" and field beginnings are a single shift ([csveee](https://db.in.tum.de/~ellmann/papers/csveee.pdf)). Quotes become a validation-only side channel. Per 64-byte block, the scanner does six `vpcmpeqb`/`vpmovmskb` pairs to build comma, quote and newline masks. Bitwise checks then confirm three things: each quote sits next to a separator, quotes pair within a field, and no `\n\n` occurs. A tzcnt loop over the newline bits counts commas per line. With about 5.8 separators per 64 B, this data is in the sparse regime where csveee found a plain scalar tzcnt extraction loop beats simdjson's unrolled one. The research notes estimate about **0.5–0.8 cycles/byte in isolation and a realistic 2–3 GB/s per core** once the consuming parsers are attached. That is an extrapolation from Zen 5 and Skylake figures, not a Cascade Lake measurement. Scanning the ~5.5 GB at that speed on 4 cores takes about 0.45–0.7 s, against an estimated 1.7–2.7 s at scalar `csv`-crate speeds. The realistic saving inside V2's fused loop is **about 0.6–1.2 s at 60M** across the event and click phases.

The simpler alternatives fall short. The `simd-csv` crate uses memchr-style searches, but it "has no concept of beginning of field" and can mis-handle stray mid-field quotes, so it cannot be a strict validator ([medialab/simd-csv](https://github.com/medialab/simd-csv)). Portable `std::simd` is still nightly-only on rustc 1.97. Hand-written `std::arch` AVX2 intrinsics inline directly under the existing `x86-64-v3` target, which, notably, lacks `pclmulqdq`. A six-step shift-XOR ladder does the prefix XOR instead, and the sparse quote mask (about 2.3 quote bytes per 64 B) lets an `if q == 0` branch skip it in most blocks.

The correctness risk is real and specific. The scanner must reproduce the malformed-line rules exactly: quote placement, empty lines, field count, and a final line without `\n`. It must also never load past the mapping end, which can SIGBUS on a page boundary. Mitigation is a padded tail or scalar epilogue, plus differential fuzzing against the existing `csv::split` with quotes at offsets 31, 32, 63 and 64.

Timestamps are the other half of per-row cost. There are about 135M of them at 60M: event `ts` and `ingest_ts`, plus click `ts`. Lemire's SSE fixed-format parser uses **65 instructions and about 7.9 ns per stamp, against 700 instructions and 46 ns for `strptime`** on Ice Lake ([Lemire 2023](https://lemire.me/blog/2023/07/01/parsing-time-stamps-faster-with-simd-instructions/)). Several further pieces fit together:

- **Validation and conversion in one pass.** Subtracting `'0'`, pairing digits with `_mm_maddubs_epi16`, and checking per-lane upper limits validates and converts all fields together. The published code checks only upper bounds, so a strict validator must add month ≥ 1 and day ≥ 1.
- **Day-number conversion.** Neri–Schneider's day-number formula takes about **1.3 ns against 2.2 ns for libc++/Hinnant-style code and 3.75 ns for glibc** ([Neri & Schneider](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2102.06959)).
- **Leap year.** A branchless check runs at 0.69 ns against 2.61 ns for the branchy version on random years ([Hüffner](https://hueffner.de/falk/blog/a-leap-year-check-in-three-instructions.html)).
- **Month length.** `30 | (m ^ (m >> 3))` gives the length of every month except February ([libstdc++](https://inbox.sourceware.org/libstdc++/20210224185406.GB3008@redhat.com)).
- **F3 epoch values and prices.** These use simdjson's 5-op digit validator and Lemire's 13-instruction, 3-multiply eight-digit SWAR combine ([simdjson](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/simdjson/simdjson/master/include/simdjson/generic/numberparsing.h); [Lemire 2022](https://lemire.me/blog/2022/01/21/swar-explained-parsing-eight-digits/)). Overflow handling follows simdjson: count significant digits after stripping leading zeros, and range-check exactly at 19.

The notes estimate the current scalar path at 15–40 ns per stamp and a vectorized one at 5–10 ns. That works out to **about 0.3–0.6 s of wall time at 60M**. These figures are extrapolated, not measured on the target. The traps are calendar edges (Feb 29 in non-leap years, month 00, day 00), F2 offsets, and F3 overflow with arbitrarily many leading zeros.

Two adjacent choices should stay as they are. AVX-512 is not worth it here. On Skylake-SP-class parts, 512-bit instructions drop the core from 3.2 to 2.4 GHz licenses, and it then takes about 650 µs to recover ([Travis Downs](https://travisdowns.github.io/blog/2020/01/17/avxfreq1.html)). Cascade Lake's penalty reaches "up to 900 MHz" ([ComputerBase](https://www.computerbase.de/2019-04/intel-cascade-lake-sp-lcc-hcc-xcc-avx-512-takt/)), and classification is only about 0.1 cycle/byte of the work anyway. The only AVX-512 variant worth an A/B test is 256-bit AVX-512VL k-mask compares behind runtime detection. Hyperthreading should stay on: the branchy validation and timestamp code is where SMT helps, and published suite-wide SMT gains average about 11% ([Phoronix](https://www.phoronix.com/review/intel-xeon-678x-ht/3)).

## Cache-sized partitions turn dedup and sort from DRAM-bound to L2-bound

The database literature agrees on how to partition in memory. Count first, prefix-sum to exact output offsets, then scatter through **one-cache-line software write-combining (SWWC) buffers per partition**, flushed with streaming stores. With SWWC, the useful fan-out is "bounded by the number of cache lines in the fast core-private cache, rather than the TLB entries", and the measured optima are **10–12 radix bits** ([Polychroniou & Ross](http://www.cs.columbia.edu/~orestis/sigmod14I.pdf)) to **14 bits in a single pass** ([Schuh et al.](https://faculty.cc.gatech.edu/~jarulraj/courses/4420-s19/papers/19-hashjoins/schuh-sigmod2016.pdf)). Choosing the wrong number of bits can cost up to 2.5×. Done this way, partitioning runs at about 97% of memcpy speed ([Müller et al.](https://ae.iti.kit.edu/2531.php)).

V2 uses 8 bits and per-split `Vec` push with doubling ([src/v2/mod.rs:82](../src/v2/mod.rs)). Doubling re-copies each bucket about log₂(n) times, which roughly doubles the write traffic on 2.9 GB of `EvRaw`. rayon's `fold` also creates one accumulator per *split*, not per thread, so each extra split adds another 256 partially filled Vecs ([rayon MinLen](https://docs.rs/rustc-rayon/latest/rayon/iter/struct.MinLen.html)). The sessions phase then copies every bucket's slices once more into a fresh contiguous Vec ([src/v2/mod.rs:179–184](../src/v2/mod.rs)).

The cheapest fix comes first. Bound the fold to one accumulator set per thread and reserve each buffer from file-size-based row estimates; this is worth about **0.2–0.4 s** for 2–3 hours of work. The full Schuh-style count-then-scatter into one exact-offset array per bucket also removes the sessions-phase gather. It saves another 0.15–0.3 s and makes every bucket a single slice that can be sorted in place.

Dedup should then stop probing DRAM. hashbrown is already a Swiss table, so switching table designs (F14, Abseil) changes nothing material ([Abseil design notes](https://abseil.io/about/design/swisstables)). Footprint is what matters. DuckDB, HyPer and Swiss tables all compare a short stored hash or tag first and verify the full key only on a match ([DuckDB](https://duckdb.org/2022/03/07/aggregate-hashtable.html); [Leis et al.](https://db.in.tum.de/~leis/papers/morsels.pdf)). V2 already hashes `event_id` to choose a bucket, so the record can carry that 64-bit hash into a `hashbrown::HashTable` of `u32` indices into the bucket slice. That cuts each slot from 64 B to 4–8 B, and the remaining hash bits pick the slot.

Combined with a 16-way sub-partition inside each bucket, which gives about 15k keys per table, each table fits in about 128–512 KB of L2. Full-`u128` verification must stay: trusting a 64-bit hash alone has about a 10⁻⁴ chance of a collision somewhere in 60M keys. If tables stay DRAM-resident anyway, group prefetching or AMAC gives **2.8–3.8× on hash probes** ([Kocberber et al.](https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol9/p252-kocberber.pdf)). The same batching applies to the `people.person()` lookups that run once per click and once per surviving event ([src/v2/mod.rs:111, 154](../src/v2/mod.rs)). How much that saves depends on the size of the linked-ID map, which the notes do not measure.

The dedup gain estimate is **0.5–1.0 s at 60M**, held at low confidence until the 20M null result for 10 bits is re-tested with the scatter fixed. Pre-dedup in the parse loop does not pay. Müller's analysis says early aggregation only helps when output is small, and at 3% duplicates it saves at most 3% of writes while adding a probe to every row.

Sorting shows the same cache cliff, and here there is a direct local measurement on the real record layout. The benchmark used 58.9M 32-byte `Ev` records on 4 cores × 2 threads, on Zen 4 rather than the target ([sorting notes](../research_notes/Rust%20pipeline%20performance%20techniques/sorting.md)):

| Bucket count (bytes each) | Current `sort_unstable` on (u128, u32) | Densify + packed u64 radix + gather | Counting scatter by person + per-person ts sort |
|---|---|---|---|
| 256 (7.4 MB) | **470 ms** | 636–661 ms | 523–546 ms |
| 1,024 (1.84 MB) | 262 ms | 385–461 ms | 309–390 ms |
| 4,096 (460 KB) | 266 ms | **105–128 ms** | 142–175 ms |

At V2's current bucket size, the existing `sort_unstable` is the best option, and every "faster" radix approach loses because four cores stream through DRAM at the same time. Shrinking the sort unit **alone cuts 470 ms to 266 ms with no new dependency**. Adding a dense per-bucket person index, a packed `pidx|ts|row` u64 key and a radix sort brings it to about 110 ms. That radix sort was `voracious_radix_sort`, which would need dependency approval, or else a hand-written LSD pass. For clicks, a radix sort on the packed key plus a linear tie-fix by `click_id` ran **2× faster** than the current three-field sort on one core.

The person scatter after dedup should therefore target about 4,096 person partitions directly, through SWWC buffers. That avoids the nb² Vec problem: 4,096 buffers of 128 B is about 512 KB, which fits in L2. The projected sort-side saving is **about 0.3–0.45 s at 60M**.

There is one correctness subtlety. The session walk merge-joins events and clicks by comparing `person` values ([src/v2/sessions.rs:187–197](../src/v2/sessions.rs)), so a dense person index must preserve `u128` order in both arrays. One way is to assign it by sorting each partition's unique persons, which costs a negligible sort of about 7.8k elements. Whale persons also break the 64-bit key budget. A 1.1M-row bucket needs 67 bits, so the fallback is a `(u64, u32)` pair or scheduling the biggest buckets first.

## Configuration fixes buy 0.3–0.8 s for an afternoon

The Parquet writer uses the `parquet` crate's defaults ([src/output.rs:89](../src/output.rs)). That means **dictionary encoding on every column and page-level min/max statistics on every value**. In a local single-threaded benchmark of a 2.2M-row `user_daily`, turning both off cut encode time from **123.5 ms to 51.8 ms**. Keeping the dictionary only on `top_category` gave **53.1 ms** and a file of 91.8 MB instead of 37.4 MB ([parquet notes](../research_notes/Rust%20pipeline%20performance%20techniques/parquet_output.md)). The high-cardinality `user_id` dictionary overflows its 1 MiB page limit within about 100k values and falls back to PLAIN anyway, so its hashing is wasted ([parquet-rs writer source](https://arrow.apache.org/rust/src/parquet/column/writer/mod.rs.html)). This is about a one-hour change with no schema impact, worth roughly **50–150 ms at 60M**. Removing the per-value `ByteArray` allocation ([src/output.rs:97](../src/output.rs)) or hand-writing a PLAIN writer gets closer to the measured 31 ms PLAIN floor, but each saves well under 100 ms and adds format risk. Switching counts to INT32 shrinks files by about 22% but changes the schema that D10 fixes, and `PARQUET_2_0` delta encodings were **4× slower** to write.

Transparent huge pages are the cheapest memory-side bet. THP applies only to anonymous memory, not to the CSV mappings ([kernel THP doc](https://docs.kernel.org/admin-guide/mm/transhuge.html)). For V2's 3–6 GB of `EvRaw`, `Ev`, `Ck` and hash-table buffers, THP cuts page faults 512× and extends TLB reach from about 6 MB to about 3 GB. TLB-bound workloads gained **1.63× with plentiful huge pages but only 1.02× under memory pressure** ([NSF paper](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10578888)). The switch is `MIMALLOC_ALLOW_LARGE_OS_PAGES=1` in the Dockerfile, or `madvise(MADV_HUGEPAGE)` on the big buffers ([mimalloc options](https://microsoft.github.io/mimalloc/environment.html)), plus `MIMALLOC_PURGE_DELAY` raised so mid-run frees stay committed for reuse. The payoff hinges on an unknown: the host's `transparent_hugepage/enabled` setting. It is zero if the host is set to `never`, and partly already captured if it is `always`. The estimate is 150–500 ms.

At exit, `main` returns normally ([src/main.rs](../src/main.rs)), so every Rust destructor runs before the kernel tears down the address space. `std::process::exit(0)` after closing the Parquet writers skips the user-space part ([std docs](https://doc.rust-lang.org/stable/std/process/fn.exit.html)). The kernel's `exit_mmap` still runs. The 1BRC fork trick that hides it does not help in a container, whose lifetime includes the child ([1BRC #138](https://github.com/gunnarmorling/1brc/discussions/138)). The expected gain is small, about 10–100 ms, but it takes 30 minutes.

PGO with a committed `.profdata` file fits the 10-minute build, because only the `-Cprofile-use` compile runs in Docker ([rustc book](https://doc.rust-lang.org/rustc/profile-guided-optimization.html)). Published Rust gains, however, are about 1% on rustc itself ([Kobzol](https://kobzol.github.io/rust/cargo/2023/07/28/rust-cargo-pgo.html)). The 3–8% the notes infer for branchy parsing is unproven and below scorer noise, so PGO belongs last, after the hot code has stopped changing.

Several things do not pay. With a warm cache, the read mechanism is worth at most low hundreds of milliseconds. Fault-around maps 16 pages per fault, so 5.5 GB costs about 84k faults, and per-page fault cost is about 0.19 µs on XFS ([c-blosc2 #880](https://github.com/Blosc/c-blosc2/pull/880)). io_uring loses to plain `pread` on warm data ([io-uring-bench](https://github.com/nazq/io-uring-bench)). Static linking saves under 1 ms. A cold cache is the real exposure: n2-8 caps Persistent Disk reads at **800 MiB/s, a 6.6 s floor for 5.5 GB, and a 100 GiB pd-balanced disk gives about 31 s** ([GCP PD performance](https://docs.cloud.google.com/compute/docs/disks/performance)). V2's early `MADV_WILLNEED` on every file is the right insurance. Synchronous `MAP_POPULATE` would be wrong, since it serializes I/O before parsing starts.

## Conclusion

V2 at 20M is mostly compute-bound, but at 60M it becomes memory-hierarchy-bound. Every per-bucket structure crosses from "fits in L3" to "lives in DRAM", which is why dedup and sort will scale worse than 3× and why the null result at 10 bucket bits misleads. That result came from a scatter whose cost grows as nb², not from the cache effect it was meant to test. The highest-value structural move is therefore not a faster hash table or sort algorithm. It is a single scatter design, using count, prefix-sum and SWWC with a carried hash, that makes partition count a free parameter. Dedup and sort can then each run on L2-sized units. The parse-side wins (SIMD scan, SWAR timestamps) are independent of that design and can be built in parallel.

Two things should be measured before more work is spent. First, check the scorer-like VM's THP mode and cold-versus-warm page cache, because either can move the result by more than any idea below. Second, split the parse phase's ~660 cycles per row between scanning, timestamps and scatter with `perf`. The 0.9–1.6 s parse estimate rests on extrapolated cycle counts, not on a Cascade Lake measurement.
