# Shrink tuples and pages to approach 3x

The external evidence supports getting the 60M run from **7.8 s down to about 2.8–3.7 s locally (roughly 2.1–2.8x)**. A full 3x (about 2.6 s, or about 3 s on the scorer once container start is added) is reachable only if every major lever lands at the good end of its range. The published honest floor for this job on 4 Cascade Lake cores is **about 1.5–2.0 s of compute**, so 3x sits just above physics rather than beyond it. The largest single lever is not a faster hash table. It is **narrower tuples**: dedup today scatters 48-byte records into 256 DRAM-sized hashbrown tables, which is outside the ≤32-byte envelope where radix partitioning pays. A 16–24-byte (key, ingest|idx) radix dedup into 2,048–4,096 L2-sized partitions, followed by a loser bitmap and one streaming person scatter, plausibly takes dedup from 2.7 s to 0.6–1.0 s and also removes a wide scatter from the parse phase. The second lever is the kernel. 12.4 CPU-s of system time at 60M is about 23% of all CPU, and published page-fault costs explain only about 0.5 CPU-s of it, so most of that time has some other cause that only a measurement on the target can find. Transparent huge pages cut exit teardown about 10x and faulting about 2x. The 1BRC subprocess trick for skipping munmap **does not work under Docker**, because the kernel makes a PID namespace's init wait for every process in it to finish its own teardown. A 0.27 s time at 20M including container start is **not honestly achievable**. Container start alone costs 0.15–0.6 s, and an honest 20M run lands around 0.7–1.2 s end to end.

## A 3x target sits just above the published floor

The best per-step evidence gives a lower bound for 60M rows on 4 physical cores of about 1.5–2.0 s when phases are fused, and about 1.8–3.0 s when they run serially:

| Step | Floor estimate | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Parse (5.4 GB, tokenize + minimal typing) | ≥ 0.75–1.1 s | csveee's 1.8 GB/s per thread with quoting, measured on Zen 5 and derated for Cascade Lake |
| Timestamp conversion | ~0.15 s | 7.9 ns per stamp with SIMD |
| Dedup 60M | ~0.3–0.6 s | thin-tuple radix design (see the dedup section) |
| Group by person + sort | ~0.3–0.7 s | DuckDB's 4-thread sort rate |
| Sessionize + Parquet | ~0.2–0.5 s | linear scans; small output |

The best published CPU CSV tokenizer, csveee, parses **1.8 GB/s per thread with quoting and 2.1 GB/s without**. That rate only counts records, and converting every column to a type made it **4.7x slower** ([Ellmann & Neumann, PVLDB 19](https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol19/p3579-ellmann.pdf)). Lemire's SIMD timestamp parser takes about **7.9 ns per stamp against 46 for `strptime`**, with full validation ([Lemire 2023](https://lemire.me/blog/2023/07/01/parsing-time-stamps-faster-with-simd-instructions/)). DuckDB sorts 100M random integers in **1.216 s on 4 threads**, about 82M keys/s ([DuckDB sort redesign](https://duckdb.org/2025/09/24/sorting-again)). Its general-purpose high-cardinality group-by (100M rows into 100M groups) runs at about **4.4M rows/s per physical core** on Ice Lake ([db-benchmark time.csv](https://duckdblabs.github.io/db-benchmark/time.csv)). At that rate our dedup alone would take about 3.4 s, so today's 7.8 s is already competitive with a general engine. Only a specialized, fused pipeline gets well below it.

Against that floor, our parse runs at **about 0.40–0.48 GB/s per physical core**, 3–4x below csveee's tokenize-only rate ([local roofline](../research/perf2/01-roofline.md)). Part of that gap is real work: 77-byte rows, three timestamp formats, malformed-line rules, and records written to memory. So the recoverable headroom in parse is about 2x, not 4x. Dedup at 2.7 s and sessions at 1.4 s are each 3–5x above their floors, which is where most of the recoverable time sits. Adding the best ideas below gives a realistic local budget of roughly **parse 1.2–1.6 s, dedup plus person scatter 0.6–1.0 s, sessions 0.5–0.8 s, write 0.2–0.3 s, and about 0.15–0.25 s less in kernel time**, for **2.8–3.7 s**. That is about 2.5x at the midpoint. Getting to 3x needs the low end of every range, or a win from the unexplained system time.

Two facts on the scoring side matter. First, the score includes container start, which costs **554–568 ms** for a warm `docker run` on a 2-vCPU Azure VM and barely depends on image size ([arXiv 2602.15214](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.15214v1)). Other measurements give **391 ms** ([Iron.io](https://blog.iron.io/the-overhead-of-docker-run/)) and about **159 ms** for `crun` alone ([Red Hat](https://www.redhat.com/en/blog/introduction-crun)). A local check in this research measured 217–419 ms with `--network none` and about 543 ms with the default bridge network. That fixed cost makes 3x on the *score* harder than 3x on the binary. Second, the reported **0.27 s at 20M** cannot be honest. After at least about 0.15–0.2 s of container start, at most about 0.1 s remains for 1.8 GB of CSV. That is about 4.5 GB/s per core, **2.5x csveee's tokenize-only Zen 5 rate**, before any dedup, sort or Parquet. The cluster of about 15 teams at 0.29–0.31 s ([round-2 brief](../research/perf2/README.md)) fits dataset-specific shortcuts: skipping fields, precomputed outputs, or caching across runs. An honest 20M run is about **0.5–0.7 s of compute plus 0.2–0.5 s of start, so 0.7–1.2 s**.

## 1BRC's front-end tricks transfer; its aggregation and exit tricks do not

The One Billion Row Challenge winner ran **1.535 s for about 12–13 GB on 8 EPYC 7502P cores**, roughly 1 GB/s per core end to end, from a RAM disk ([1BRC README](https://github.com/gunnarmorling/1brc)). The best documented Rust entry reached about **2 GB/s per core**: 1.05 s on 6 cores at 3.6 GHz ([Groot Koerkamp](https://curiouscoding.nl/posts/1brc/)). In rows, the winners process about 650M rows/s against our about 21M. Rows per second is the wrong comparison, though, because a 1BRC row is about 14 bytes with one delimiter, a fixed-format number, and a lookup in a 413-entry table that fits in L1/L2. Even the 1BRC field was sensitive to key count: moving to 10K keys made artsiomkorzun's entry **1.587 → 2.957 s** and reordered the leaderboard ([Morling results](https://www.morling.dev/blog/1brc-results-are-in/)). Our dedup table has 60M keys, about 4,000 times the 10K-key case.

Groot Koerkamp's step log ranks the techniques that generalize. Parallelism gave about 6x. Dropping per-row allocation and `String`, parsing integers, and using a cheap hash gave about 2.5x together. The `memchr` crate's SIMD search took one stage from **42 to 29 s**, while libc `memchr` was *slower* than a naive loop because it is not inlined, and hand-written 32-lane SIMD did no better than the crate. Branchless parsing took **2.0 to 1.7 s**. Four independent chunks interleaved per thread, written with `macro_rules!` "since the compiler did not do it reliably", took **1.34 to 1.05 s** and raised IPC from 2.20 to 2.95. `get_unchecked` was worth only about 2% and introduced UB that later had to be fixed ([Groot Koerkamp](https://curiouscoding.nl/posts/1brc/)). On the official box, 2 MiB segments claimed from an atomic counter "to make all threads finish at the same time" took **2.019 to 1.893 s** ([1BRC timeline](https://tivrfoa.github.io/java/benchmark/performance/2024/02/05/1BRC-Timeline.html)). The winner combines all of these: SWAR delimiter search, a branchless one-multiply number parse, and three cursors per thread ([thomaswue source](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/gunnarmorling/1brc/main/src/main/java/dev/morling/onebrc/CalculateAverage_thomaswue.java)). Late-stage C++ work shows how fast returns diminish: branchless parsing about 1%, page-cache pre-touch about 3%, and pinning with `numactl` **10–20% slower** ([1BRC #138](https://github.com/gunnarmorling/1brc/discussions/138)).

For us, the memchr item is already in place (S3-4), and chunks are already pulled dynamically, though at 32 MiB rather than 2 MiB ([critical-path notes](../research/perf2/06-critical-path.md)). What still transfers is a branchless SWAR/SSE timestamp parse with a cheap format discriminator, an AVX2 64-byte structural pass that builds comma, quote and newline masks together, and **2–4 interleaved cursors per thread**. Expect less than 1BRC's 20% from interleaving, because our per-row work is longer and branchier, and SMT may already capture part of the gain (see the hyperthreading section). Cascade Lake has fast PDEP/PEXT, so the Zen 1/2 penalty Groot Koerkamp hit does not apply. Perfect hashing, "one decimal digit", and skipping counts are 1BRC-specific shortcuts and are excluded here.

The subprocess trick is a clear no. In 1BRC, the parent re-launches itself as a `--worker`, forwards the child's output, and returns without `waitFor()`. hyperfine timed only the parent, so the 12–13 GB unmap fell outside the measurement ([thomaswue source](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/gunnarmorling/1brc/main/src/main/java/dev/morling/onebrc/CalculateAverage_thomaswue.java)). It was worth something there: munmap cost **about 180 ms** of a 1.05 s Rust run, and adopting the trick moved one C++ entry from **0.319 to 0.281 s** ([1BRC #138](https://github.com/gunnarmorling/1brc/discussions/138)). In a container it cannot help. `do_exit()` runs `exit_mm()` before `exit_notify()`, so a process tears down its own address space before anyone is told it exited ([kernel/exit.c](https://github.com/torvalds/linux/blob/master/kernel/exit.c)). When a PID namespace's init exits, `zap_pid_ns_processes()` SIGKILLs every remaining task and waits until the namespace's PID count drops back to zero ([pid_namespace.c](https://github.com/torvalds/linux/blob/master/kernel/pid_namespace.c)). A child holding the memory is therefore killed and waited for, and its teardown stays on the measured path. The same holds with tini as PID 1. QEMU's `clone(CLONE_VM)` async-teardown helper fails in the same way: libvirt still blocks until the helper exits ([Berrangé](https://lore.kernel.org/all/Yuv6uhuxjQHawmeL@redhat.com/)). The only escape is to leave the PID namespace (for example `--pid=host`), which is a change to the harness, not to our code.

## Thin-tuple radix dedup beats atomic tables and sorting

Three designs compete at 10^7–10^8 keys. Published numbers rank them clearly, though none were measured on a 4-core Cascade Lake VM.

| Design | Published rate | Estimated dedup time for 60–64M keys on 4c/8t |
|---|---|---|
| Today: 48 B records, 256 hashbrown tables (~34 MB each) | — | 2.7 s measured |
| Global lock-free linear-probing table ("folklore"), no prefetch | 417 Mops on 64 Skylake-SP threads (~6.5M per thread) | 0.8–1.3 s, plus 1.5–2 GB of table faults and a confirm pass |
| Same with batched prefetch (DRAMHiT) | 792 Mops (~12.4M per thread), at the DRAM limit | 0.6–0.8 s if the VM sustains ~15 GB/s of random read-modify-write |
| Thin 16–24 B radix partition (2^11–2^12) + L2-resident per-partition tables | radix join 196M tuples/s on 8 Sandy Bridge cores | 0.3–0.5 s |
| Full radix sort of (key, payload) pairs | ~23M tuples/s per core for 32-bit keys | 1.0–1.6 s |

The shared-table evidence comes from growt and DRAMHiT. A pre-sized, non-growing linear-probing table over word-sized slots is "an order of magnitude faster than the best more general tables". Folly, Junction, TBB, libcuckoo and RCU tables are **2.5–64x slower** when growing ([Maier et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1601.04017)). On 2-socket Skylake-SP, the same core as Cascade Lake, that table tops out at **417 Mops for a 16 GB table**, and batched software prefetch nearly doubles it to **792 Mops**, which matches the DRAM transaction limit of 1.3 line reads plus a write-back per insert ([DRAMHiT](https://mars-research.github.io/doc/2023-eurosys-dramhit.pdf)). Rust's general concurrent maps are not candidates either: papaya's author says it "falls short in update-heavy workloads" ([papaya benchmarks](https://github.com/ibraheemdev/papaya/blob/master/BENCHMARKS.md)). The global table has two further costs for our semantics. `AtomicU64::fetch_min` is a `lock cmpxchg` loop on x86, and it is deterministic only if ties break on an input-order index. More importantly, no thread knows it holds the winner until every insert has finished, so materializing needs a second probe per row. Our own dedup analysis reached the same conclusion: −1.0 to +0.3 s for 12–16 h of unsafe code ([dedup-arch notes](../research/perf2/02-dedup-arch.md)).

Radix partitioning fits our workload because all the conditions the literature sets hold here. Bandle et al. find partitioning **"workable" for payloads ≤32 B and "beneficial" only at ≤16 B**, with low skew and a build side much larger than the LLC. The radix join "completely loses the advantage once the tuple size exceeds 32 B", and partitioning 24 B instead of 40 B with late materialization raised throughput from **153M to 341M tuples/s** ([Bandle et al.](https://db.in.tum.de/~bandle/papers/bandle-partitionVsNonPartition.pdf)). With equal-sized inputs, the optimized radix join beat the no-partition join by **about 3.5x on Intel** ([Balkesen et al.](https://www.comp.nus.edu.sg/~hebs/course/cs6284/papers/Balkesen-icde2013.pdf)). With one cache-line software write-combining buffer per partition, the best single-pass fanout is **10–12 bits** ([Polychroniou & Ross](http://www.cs.columbia.edu/~orestis/sigmod14I.pdf)), and partitioning runs at **about 97% of memcpy speed** ([Müller et al. summary](https://rmarcus.info/dbscholar/papers/hc6fc8717e8f0c055)). Our current design breaks the tuple-width rule twice: 48-byte records, and 256 partitions whose tables grow to about 34 MB each, 34 times a 1 MB L2.

The concrete design is as follows. During parse, write each `EvRaw` sequentially into the chunk's own buffer instead of scattering it by event hash. Alongside it, emit a thin tuple through SWWC buffers into 2,048–4,096 partitions. The thin tuple is either 16 B (u64 hash, then ingest in 38 bits and a 26-bit global row index), with full u128 verification read from the record on a hash match, or 24 B (the key as `[u64; 2]` plus `u32` ingest and `u32` index), which needs no extra read. At 64M / 4,096 ≈ 16K tuples per partition, an open-addressing table at about 50% fill takes about 512 KB and stays in L2 with no atomics. Each partition writes loser indices into a 64M-bit (8 MB) bitmap. A single streaming pass over the `EvRaw` buffers then skips losers, does the person lookup, and scatters survivors into person partitions, a pass the pipeline needs anyway. Random-gathering winners instead would cost 62M DRAM misses. Determinism is exact: ties break on the global row index, and with a 16 B tuple every 64-bit hash match is checked against the full key, since roughly 10^-4 collisions are expected across 62M keys. Correctness traps from our rules carry over unchanged: only copies dropped as malformed during parse are excluded, a parsed copy that later fails validation can still win and is dropped afterwards, and equal-ingest copies are identical. A full radix sort is strictly more work than this. IPS2Ra-class parallel radix sorts "do not scale well" with thread count ([arXiv 2401.00710](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2401.00710)), and the Rust crates publish no relevant numbers ([voracious_sort](https://github.com/lakwet/voracious_sort)).

The 0.3–0.5 s estimate is extrapolated from Sandy Bridge, using Balkesen's 196M tuples/s, and it assumes the VM gets enough memory bandwidth. A useful cross-check uses Little's law with about 12 fill buffers per core and about 95 ns of DRAM latency. That gives about 125M random line fills/s per core, so even a perfectly prefetched global table needs about **0.25 s just to touch 64M × ~2 lines**. A radix design moves about 1–1.5 GB sequentially instead. The compact exact-key `HashMap<u128, u32>` card from the round-2 notes (300–500 ms, 3–4 h, low risk) is a sensible stepping stone, but it is not the destination ([dedup-arch notes](../research/perf2/02-dedup-arch.md)). The person probes in the survivor pass, estimated at 0.5–0.9 s of dedup wall, should use batched prefetch. On uniform hash probes, group prefetching and AMAC gave **2.8x and 4.3x single-thread** gains ([Kocberber et al.](https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol9/p252-kocberber.pdf)).

## Kernel memory costs: THP pays; process tricks do not

Per-page costs are well measured. Write-faulting 4 GiB of private anonymous memory with 4K pages took **708 ms, about 675 ns per page**. `MADV_POPULATE_WRITE` took 573 ms. 2 MiB pages populated the same 4 GiB in about **356 ms, roughly 12 GB/s** ([LKML MADV_POPULATE](https://lkml.iu.edu/hypermail/linux/kernel/2105.1/03598.html)). Reading a fresh buffer before writing it costs about 1.5x a pure write (1,054 ms), so `vec![0; n]` followed by reads is the worst pattern. Teardown is just as clear. A July 2026 RFC measured exit for a killed process at **18 ms for 1 GB, 74 ms for 4 GB and 282 ms for 16 GB** with 4K pages on bare metal. The 16 GB case took **426 ms inside a QEMU guest and 27.7 ms with THP**. A 4 GB tmpfs mapping took 35 ms. The teardown runs synchronously in the dying task ([LKML RFC](https://lkml.iu.edu/2607.2/06062.html); [patchew thread](https://patchew.org/linux/20260719185409.409685-1-adi.sharma@zohomail.in/)). Maintainers pushed back on deferring it, saying the work "is necessary" ([LKML reply](https://lkml.iu.edu/2607.2/08437.html)). Scaling those numbers to about 7.4 GB of anonymous memory plus a 5.4 GB file mapping on a VM gives **about 200 ms + 50–75 ms ≈ 250–270 ms of exit teardown**. That matches the local gap between native start-to-exit and the internal total: **318 ms at 60M against 57 ms at 20M** ([local profile](../research/perf2/00-profile.md)). THP on the anonymous buffers would cut the anonymous part to about 20 ms.

The more important finding is how much time this does *not* explain. The 60M profile shows **12.4 CPU-s of system time and about 826k minor faults** ([local profile](../research/perf2/00-profile.md)). At 550–675 ns per fault, those faults account for only about **0.5–0.6 CPU-s**. Even zeroing the full 7.2 GB of `RssAnon` at about 10 GB/s adds well under 1 CPU-s. That leaves more than 10 CPU-s, about 1.3 s of wall time spread over 8 threads, with no measured cause. The likely candidates are in the profile itself, but they are unconfirmed. The run was taken on a laptop with **13–14 of 15 GiB of swap in use and 5 GiB free**, so direct reclaim and compaction probably inflated it. mimalloc's page-commit path (`mi_page_free_list_extend`, 6.9% of user samples) and purge/recommit cycles redo zap-and-fault work. On kernels before 6.4, any allocator `mmap`/`munmap` takes `mmap_lock` for writing and stalls every faulting thread, while per-VMA locks from 6.4 onward remove that bottleneck for anonymous faults ([LWN 906852](https://lwn.net/Articles/906852/)). Idle rayon workers that spin on yield are another possible source of system time; that is a hypothesis, not a sourced finding. This bucket may be partly a local artifact, which would make the scorer faster than local, or it may be real and the cheapest second-largest lever. Only `perf`, `strace -c` and `/proc/vmstat` on a scorer-like VM can tell.

THP behaves the same way under Docker as on the host. The mode is set host-wide and Docker cannot change it, but `madvise(MADV_HUGEPAGE)` from inside the container works whenever the host mode is `madvise` or `always` ([kernel THP doc](https://docs.kernel.org/7.2/_sources/admin-guide/mm/transhuge.rst.txt)). GKE documents `madvise` for both enabled and defrag as "the default kernel configuration" ([GKE enum](https://docs.cloud.google.com/python/docs/reference/container/latest/google.cloud.container_v1.types.LinuxNodeConfig.TransparentHugepageEnabled)). The dev laptop runs `always`. That is a measurement trap: local runs may get huge pages the scorer only gets if we ask for them, so local gains from THP can overstate the scorer, and the scorer can lose time the laptop never pays. khugepaged runs too slowly to matter for a process that lives a few seconds. `MADV_COLLAPSE` can create THPs even under `never`, but only after the 4K faults have already been paid. mimalloc's THP behavior is not settled. One commit sets `allow_large_os_pages` to 2 on Linux, meaning THP only ([mimalloc f8c6acd](https://github.com/microsoft/mimalloc/commit/f8c6acdf77945fc124da0fa91b9d73b96c6569c3)). The documentation also lists `MIMALLOC_ALLOW_THP` and disagrees with itself about defaults ([mimalloc options](https://microsoft.github.io/mimalloc/environment.html)). Combined with the earlier A/B that recorded no win, the robust approach is to bypass the allocator: put the few huge buffers (`EvRaw`, thin tuples, person-partition output) in one 2 MB-aligned `mmap` arena with `MADV_HUGEPAGE`, fall back to plain Vecs automatically, and check `AnonHugePages` in `/proc/self/smaps_rollup` and `thp_fault_fallback` in `/proc/vmstat`. THP also extends TLB reach to about 3 GB, which matters because every random probe into a multi-GB table otherwise takes a page walk, and Cascade Lake has only 2 page walkers per core ([Chips and Cheese](https://old.chipsandcheese.com/2022/06/07/sunny-cove-intels-lost-generation/)). Beyond THP, prefault with `MADV_POPULATE_WRITE` from an idle thread during the serial links/union-find step. Unmap the input in the background after the last parse, worth up to the 50–75 ms of file teardown if S3-3 does not already do it. Skip whole-file `MAP_POPULATE`: if the page cache is cold, n2-8 Persistent Disk caps reads at **800 MiB/s, a 6.6 s floor** for 5.5 GB ([GCP PD performance](https://docs.cloud.google.com/compute/docs/disks/performance)), and that would outweigh every CPU change in this report.

## Hyperthreads help naive probes and hurt cache-tuned phases

The SMT evidence is consistent: the sibling thread helps in proportion to how many cache misses each thread leaves unhidden. For radix joins on Nehalem and Sandy Bridge, "cache-efficient algorithms cannot benefit from SMT threads". Both radix implementations scaled linearly to the physical core count and then lost ground from shared caches and TLBs. On SPARC T4, 8 threads per core made the radix join *slower* than 1 ([Balkesen et al. TKDE](https://cs.uwaterloo.ca/~tozsu/publications/other/hash-joins-tkde-final.pdf)). For hash probes, the no-prefetch baseline did scale onto HT contexts, cutting AMAC's 2.5x single-thread advantage to about 1.8x. The prefetching variants gained nothing from HT because "the limit of L1-D outstanding misses" was already reached at about 10 in flight ([Kocberber et al.](https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol9/p252-kocberber.pdf)). Coroutines combined with HT still gave **1.7–2.1x** on Masstree, and the authors warn that interleaving gains are overstated when HT is disabled ([Jonathan et al.](https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol11/p1702-jonathan.pdf)). For parsing, 1BRC's mid-stage code gained **10–62%** from HT, but the fully interleaved final Rust version did not: 0.90 s on 6 threads against 0.92 s on 12 ([Groot Koerkamp](https://curiouscoding.nl/posts/1brc/)). csveee saw "diminishing or even slightly negative returns" from SMT ([csveee](https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol19/p3579-ellmann.pdf)). No study found that oversubscribing OS threads helps. A context switch costs microseconds, far more than a 100 ns miss ([Psaropoulos et al.](https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol11/p230-psaropoulos.pdf)).

Cascade Lake cores have 10–12 L1D fill buffers, shared by both siblings, and 32 superqueue entries ([Chips and Cheese](https://old.chipsandcheese.com/2022/06/07/sunny-cove-intels-lost-generation/); [ZombieLoad](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1905.05726)). That suggests a per-phase recipe:

| Phase | Threads | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Parse | 8 (test 4 once cursors are interleaved) | branchy, so HT helps until ILP fills the core |
| Thin-tuple scatter | 4 | SWWC buffers need L1 lines; siblings halve the budget, and 4,096 partitions is already at the edge |
| L2-resident dedup tables | 4 (test 8) | cache-resident work; HT evicts |
| DRAM-resident probes (person map) | 8 if naive; 4–8 with batch prefetch, about 10–16 misses in flight per core in total | per-core fill-buffer limit |
| Per-partition radix sort | 4 (test 8) | likely neutral or negative |
| Sequential walk and write | 8 | streaming |

Avoid pinning individual threads, which cost 10–20% in 1BRC ([1BRC #138](https://github.com/gunnarmorling/1brc/discussions/138)). Read the sibling map from `/sys/devices/system/cpu/*/topology/thread_siblings_list` on the VM rather than assuming it, and confirm the CPU generation with `lscpu`. Per-phase rayon pools make each 4-vs-8 A/B a one-line change. All the gains in this table are extrapolations from Nehalem through Broadwell, so they are hypotheses to measure.

## Conclusion

Taken together, the external evidence changes the picture. Dedup and sort are slow because of **record width and partition size**, not table design. Swiss tables, atomic tables and radix sorts all lose to a partition pass over narrow tuples into L2-sized pieces. The thin-tuple design also deletes one wide scatter and turns winner selection into an 8 MB bitmap, which is why it is worth more than its dedup-only estimate suggests. The second change is that **the kernel is now a first-class phase**. Exit teardown is bounded and well understood (about 250 ms, about 10x cheaper with THP, and impossible to hide inside Docker). The 12.4 CPU-s of system time is not explained by any published per-fault cost, and nobody yet knows whether the scorer pays it. That makes one hour of measurement on a scorer-like n2 VM the highest-value next step, ahead of any code change.

The honest ceiling also clarifies the target. About 2.5x is the evidence-backed expectation. 3x needs the low end of every range, and reaching the 1.5–2 s floor would need a fully fused single-pass design that the remaining schedule does not support. The 0.27 s leaders at 20M are doing something other than this job, and chasing them would mean breaking the honesty rule.
